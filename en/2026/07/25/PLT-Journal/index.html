La Salle-Pui Ching Programming Challenge journal.

Original: Chinese

Translation: Gemini

Hereinafter referred to as PLT.

Registration was required just a few days before NOI. Although PLT clashed with the HKMO training team sessions, I still decided to participate in PLT.

From my past experience competing in ACM, there's a rather strange phenomenon: a three-person team shouldn't consist entirely of top-tier players, otherwise they will underperform (who knows why). My best runs were either flying solo, or when qwertim and I teamed up with General Huang as our assistant. My absolute worst run was with Adam and ltw—both IOI team members—where we ended up dead last in HKOI. Truly hilarious.

So when Matthew invited Andy and me to form a team, it was exactly what I wanted. Both of them are juniors who excel at math in our school. Plus, Andy frequently solves ad-hoc problems that stump me; Andy and I even pulled off some insane team results back at the HKOI training camp. I didn't dare carry the older students from school, because it wouldn't feel right if I ended up writing code the entire time, and our strengths wouldn't complement each other.

Thus, the team was formed, and we successfully became the official PCMS team. We named ourselves Polynomial Laplace Transform.

Our strategy was simple. I would write code the whole time and handle all the algorithmic problems, while the two of them tackled the ad-hoc ones.

The Contest

Before the contest started, Matthew introduced us to the research content of this year's Fields Medal, and then roughly outlined a proof method for the 2D Kakeya conjecture (Note: this year's Fields Medal was for 3D). I listened with great interest. He also said he learnt how to prove Fermat's Last Theorem. Absolute legend.

The contest began. Within the first hour, we cleared 5 warmup problems and felt in great shape. Then I noticed that many teams were solving A and C, so I had Andy think about A while I focused on C.

I spent 20 minutes seriously thinking through C, while Andy was also developing some ideas for A. For C, you could just iterate through the starting points and maintain the endpoints using a set. I implemented C first, but then realized one step was flawed and I had overcomplicated things. I quickly fixed it and solved the problem.

Andy came up with a brilliant position-skipping solution, which felt incredibly smart. I suggested a greedy approach, and then realized combining them worked perfectly. We took a time penalty but successfully locked it down.

Next, we looked at J, which some people had solved. I realized it was a classic hashing practice problem. I initially wrote a map solution but got TLE, so I switched to hashing. I tried single-modulo hashing first but got WA, changed it to double-modulo and still got WA, only to finally discover a typo somewhere. Once fixed, it ACed, but bruh, we racked up so much time penalty.

At this point, I planned to tackle F, which was a harder version of a simple problem shx had set among the SMSOIers. After thinking for 5 minutes, I saw that Adam ACed F, so it was definitely doable.

I spent 20 minutes writing the code, but since it was a probability problem, it was hard to debug, so I spent another 20 minutes debugging. It felt like a terrible waste of time. Meanwhile, Andy and Matthew worked together and figured out a solution for L, which surprised me because no one had passed L yet. To be safe, I kept working on F, but it threw another TLE upon submission. I tweaked the square-root decomposition threshold, but it still TLE'd.

Then we realized I couldn't submit anything else because one of my previous submissions was stuck in Pending. At this point, there were only 5 minutes left in the contest, and we felt completely hopeless. Even when the contest ended, it was still Pending. Truly doomed.

Or so we thought?

It was announced that the contest would be extended by 10 minutes because the judging server was heavily lagging in the final 5 minutes, though writing new code was forbidden. Then, looking closely, that Pending submission actually passed!

We were saved!

In the end, we happily took RK 4. It was another classic ACM performance—most solved problems alongside the highest time penalty. The exact same thing happened at the HIT Shenzhen ACM last time.

Just made the cutoff for a Gold Award, an absolute win. I became the match MVP by coding 9 problems. Honestly, if I hadn't spent so long debugging F at the end, we could have totally cleared L and hit RK 1. But that's on me, since my coding speed is naturally slow.

Happily brought home a trophy and a medal. Took a big group photo, hung out with Adam, sang the NOI versions of "Run, Youth" and "Crossing Bridge" together, packed up, and cleared out.

The End
